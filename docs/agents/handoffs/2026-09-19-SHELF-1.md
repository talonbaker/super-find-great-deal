## 3. The room, and why every number in it is arithmetic

The interior is 14 × 10 m. **Four bays 0.5 m deep run the room's length with 1.6 m of walkable
floor between every pair and between the outer pair and the walls: 4 × 0.5 + 5 × 1.6 = 10.0 m,
which is the room's width exactly.** So the aisle centres are z = −3.15, −1.05, +1.05, +3.15 and
they are not adjustable without giving up one of the two properties the packet asked for (1.6 m
walkways, four aisles).

Along x the aisles stop at ±4.6, leaving a 2.4 m cross-aisle at each end: four 2.30 m bays per
aisle, centres −3.45 / −1.15 / +1.15 / +3.45, adjacent uprights meeting face to face.

- **Cans on aisles 0 and 2, cereal boxes on aisles 1 and 3, produce in the end-caps and the
  bins.** That is a gameplay fact rather than merchandising: SFX-1 gave each material its own
  voice, so knocking a can over in the dark tells the other player which aisle you are in.
- **Eight products per shelf, two runs of four**, the runs staggered to a different pair of bays
  on each of the three levels and alternating between the front and back half of the 0.5 m
  depth, so an aisle reads stocked from either side and from either end.
- **No back panels** (`ShelfUnit.tscn`'s header): a bay you can see and shove a can THROUGH is
  what makes an aisle a place to root around in rather than a wall with tins on it.
- **`RoomBounds` was VERIFIED, not changed.** It is the interior box x ∈ [−7, 7], y ∈ [0, 3.5],
  z ∈ [−5, 5]; the tallest thing in the room is a 1.8 m bay, the widest is a bin at x = ±6.85,
  the deepest is a bin at z = ±4.75. Everything authored is inside it.
- **`SearchPillar` STAYS.** CARRY-1 left it deletable "the moment a real shelf back does the same
  job", and no shelf back here does: the bays have none, and a 0.05 m board cannot bury a 0.44 m
  crate the way REACH-1's phase 2 needs (it seeds one at world (46, 1, 2.5) — the pillar's
  centre — and requires layer 3 to answer `InsideStatic`). Deleting it would have cost a
  replacement fixture AND an edit to another lane's suite to buy nothing.

## 4. The `Stock` container is load-bearing, and it is one character

`PropManager.AdoptAuthoredProps` hands out ids from 1000 in **ordinal node-path sort order**, so
the id of every prop in a room is a function of the NAME of every other prop in it. Nothing about
that is visible in a diff.

**`Bin_`, `Box_`, `Can_` and `Produce_` all sort BEFORE `Prop_`** — every one starts below `P`.
Authored as siblings of CARRY-1's four crates they would have renumbered all four by 126, and
`tests/Run-PlaceTest.ps1` names 1000, 1001, 1002 and 1003 in its own source. They live under a
node named **`Stock`** instead, because `S` sorts after `P`. That is the whole trick and it is
written into `SearchRoom.tscn` at the node.

Names are **zero-padded to three digits** for the same family of reason: an ordinal sort puts
`Can_10` between `Can_1` and `Can_2`, so unpadded names would be deterministic, identical on
every peer, and wrong about which object a level author was looking at.

**Both are asserted rather than commented.** `tests/unit/AuthoredPropOrderTests.cs` re-derives the
whole block from `SearchRoom.tscn`'s own node names with no engine, in milliseconds, and
`tests/Run-AuthoredPropTest.ps1` proves it on a live server and a late joiner.

**The one thing this does NOT protect against, and INT-1 should know:** a prop authored into the
**holding room** takes id 1000, because `HoldingRoom` sorts before `SearchRoom`. BTN-1's rack and
drop-off bin are exactly that. Whoever merges BTN-1 must re-read the block — `Run-AuthoredPropTest`
will say so in its first line, and `Run-PlaceTest` will go red.

## 5. The owed suite, back: `tests/Run-AuthoredPropTest.ps1` (udp/7908)

`docs/PRUNE-BACKLOG.md` has listed "the authored-prop adoption proof" as owed since the fork.
**It is not the deleted script restored**, and the difference is the point: the old one proved an
authored prop could be grabbed, thrown and settled to the same place on two peers, all of which
`Run-CarryTest` and `Run-PlaceTest` now cover on these same crates. What 130 authored props
actually put at risk is the id assignment rule.

A server, a bot present from the start, and a bot that joins **8 s late**. Five phases:

1. the adoption line's count and range on all three peers (`[props] adopted 130 authored prop(s)
   in T ms (ids 1000..1129)`, new in `PropManager`), and that the three agree;
2. all 130 ids in **each client's FIRST sample** — an authored prop is adopted locally and is
   never spawned and never streamed, so a peer that had to wait for a dump would be showing an
   empty shop;
3. the `PropKind` of every id against the five-block table;
4. the authored pose of the **first id of each block** (a rename inside a block keeps every kind
   right and moves exactly these);
5. cross-peer agreement on all 130 — kept because it is free and catches a peer that built a
   different world, but honest about what it proves here: nothing moves in this run, so a green
   reads 0.0000 m. `Run-PlaceTest` and `Run-CarryNetTest` are the suites that prove a MOVING prop
   converges.

**Proved able to fail.** `Stock/Can_000` renamed to `Stock/Can_900`:

## 6. Two other lanes' suites broke, both for the same structural reason, both fixed

Neither was a defect in that lane's subject. **The search room was an empty box when both were
written**, and a walk brain that goes in a straight line makes every piece of furniture in the
room part of somebody else's staging. Read this section before adding any fixture to that room.

### 6.1 `Run-PlaceTest` — a stocked room makes "grab the nearest thing" ambiguous

**Bot D came away holding 1013, a cereal box, instead of crate 1002.** Correctly: the crate lies
at y = 0.22 and the box at y = 0.565, so from an avatar standing at the 1.2 m arrive radius the
crate is 1.22 m away and a box on the aisle 0.94 m across is 1.02 m. Nearest-carryable is the
right rule for a player — you grab what is under your hand, and that is the whole texture of a
stocked aisle. **It is simply no longer an instruction a suite can use to name an object.**

`--carry-target-prop` already existed for CARRY-1's version of this lesson (*"walk to
(−2.5, 0.5, −2.5)" is only the same instruction as "walk to the ball" while nobody has moved the
ball*). **`SandboxAvatar.ScriptedGrabPropId` extends it one step:** with a prop named,
`FindNearestCarryable` returns THAT prop — **if it is free and inside the same `PickupRadius`**,
so the flag narrows the choice and never widens the reach, and a scripted grab that would have
been refused for distance still is. −1 for every human player and every suite that does not pass
it. The four place bots now pass it.

**Bot D also walked into `EndCap_0`**, which sat on its diagonal from Prop_2 to the +X wall;
measured, it stopped at world x = 44.45, 15 cm short of the end-cap's face, and the suite
reported a refusal that never happened. `EndCap_0` closes the second aisle instead.

**And `SearchSpawn_2`/`_3` moved twice.** First out of the fourth aisle's footprint (z = 3 is
inside a bay now) to z = 4.2 — which is a perfectly legal standing spot and turned the suite red
anyway, because an aisle then stood between the marker and the room. **The rule for a spawn
marker in this room is not "somewhere legal to stand", it is "the same walkway as the props a bot
starting there is going to want".** `_2` is now in the z-band [1.3, 2.9] with Prop_1 and `_3` in
[−2.9, −1.3] with Prop_2. Names and count untouched, which is what the spawn contract binds.

### 6.2 `Run-MaterialSfxTest` — three of its seven fixture props were inside a shelf

Its can, box and produce were seeded at world z = −3, which is now inside the first aisle's bays;
the can came to rest at (40.11, 0.07, −3.00) wedged under a board. Worse, **a bot cannot walk
from one walkway to another**: `SfxCanBot` spawned at marker 0 (35.5, 0), walked at (40, −3) and
stopped dead against the second aisle's face at (40.09, −0.65). Its grab never fired and the
suite reported `tin: prop 1 never fired TinPick on PickedUp` — a sound assertion about a pickup
that never happened.

Each driver's prop now lives in **that driver's own walkway**, matched to the spawn marker its
join order gets it (measured from the bots' own first samples, not assumed): can at z = 0, box at
z = 2.1, produce at z = −2.1; the stack and the two free-fallers, which nobody walks to, in the
+Z edge walkway at z = 4.2. Phase 2's forty-prop heap moved into the central walkway for the same
reason — a third of it was landing on shelf boards rather than on other props, which is not the
concurrency event that phase measures. **The subject did not move an inch: same seven props, same
kinds, same drop, same relative geometry in the once-per-contact stack.**

### 6.3 The structural finding, for the orchestrator

**The dressed search room is not straight-line navigable between walkways.** Four 9.2 m aisles
with no break means a bot in one walkway cannot reach another, and `MoveAndSlide` slides rather
than routes. A human player walks round the end; a scripted bot does not. This will bite every
future fixture staged in that room (TASK-1, BTN-1, HOLD-1, INT-1's ride card).

**The fix if Talon wants it is a central cross-aisle** — split each aisle into 2 + 2 bays with a
1.6 m gap at x = 0, which is what a real shop does with a 10 m run and would make the room a mesh
rather than four corridors. It is a re-layout (every product position, both end-caps, the
landmarks in `Run-AuthoredPropTest`) and it would **not** by itself fix a diagonal walk, so it is
reported rather than done. §6.1 and §6.2's rule — put a fixture in the walkway its bot spawns in
— is the one that actually works today.

## 7. The numbers

### 7.1 Prop count and server frame time — **120 products holds the bar**

`tests/Measure-ShopFloor.ps1`, passes `rest` and `shove`: the same probe, the same 1 s warm-up,
the same nearest-rank percentile rule, **one variable changed** (`--cost-shove-every`), which is
the only reason the two are comparable. A headless dedicated server, 130 authored props, two
headless bots walking, 1200 physics ticks over 20 s.

| | props | server p50 | **server p95** | peak | audits | corrections |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **at rest** | 130 | **1.481 ms** | **5.798 ms** | 20.419 ms | 0 | 0 |
| **under the shove** (130 props knocked loose every 3 s, 6 waves) | 130 | **6.604 ms** | **19.840 ms** | 21.021 ms | 598 | **178** |

**The packet's bar is p95 under 8 ms at rest, and 120 products makes it with 2.2 ms to spare.
The count does not need to be traded down.** There is headroom to raise it, but not much: the
at-rest p95 is already 72 % of the bar with the room asleep, so 120 is where I would leave it
until someone wants the density more than the margin.

The shove figure is an **upper bound and not a budget**, for `ReachCostProbe`'s own reason: a real
session never knocks every prop in the room loose every three seconds. Read it against the
at-rest row, not against 8 ms.

### 7.2 Client frame time

A client does **not** simulate a loose prop — it lerps a frozen kinematic body toward a streamed
transform — so the server's number says nothing about what 130 props cost the player watching
them. There was no client-side instrument, so `BotHarness` now samples
`Performance.Monitor.TimePhysicsProcess` into each JSONL line as `pms`, at its 5 Hz logging
cadence (a deliberately cheap spot reading, not a per-frame accumulator inside every bot in the
repo). `Measure-ShopFloor` reports p50/p95/peak from it. **Numbers in §7.5.**

### 7.3 Loose-stream bandwidth, measured rather than costed

ENet's own counters (`--net-stats`, `NetStatsLogger`), mean over the 17 seconds of each run that
had two peers connected:

| | sent | packets | recv |
|---|---|---|---|
| at rest | **9.3 kB/s** | 138 pkt/s | 14.1 kB/s |
| under the shove | **228.6 kB/s** | **3 306 pkt/s** | 14.0 kB/s |

**A 24× jump in bytes and a 24× jump in packets.** The loose stream is 30 Hz per loose prop with a
skip-unchanged gate, so 130 props all loose at once is the whole of that number, and it is the
figure to argue about if anyone wants more than 120 products. At rest the gate does its job:
9.3 kB/s with 130 props in the room is the two avatars and the round wire.

### 7.4 Adopt time

`[props] adopted 130 authored prop(s) in T ms (ids 1000..1129)`, printed on every peer:
**3.83 – 6.02 ms**, once per world build, identical on the server and on both clients. The walk is
O(nodes) and the sort O(n log n). Reported, never gated.

### 7.5 Voice budget under the shove — **the pool is saturated, and by a wider margin than SFX-1's**

Windowed host (the only peer that SIMULATES the props, so the only one where two moving bodies can
both report a contact — SFX-1's finding), two windowed first-person clients with `--log-sfx`. The
summary line comes from a CLIENT because `ActorFx.BudgetSummaryLine` is printed by
`BotHarness.Finish` and a server process never reaches it; that is SFX-1's limitation, unchanged.

| run | host `[sfx]` fires | client | fires | `PeakLive3DVoices` | `OneShotSteals` | stolen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| shove every 14 s (the committed script) | 452 | ShopEast | 382 | **18** | 305 | **79.8 %** |
| ″ | | ShopWest | 424 | **18** | 343 | **80.9 %** |
| shove every 6 s (harsher, measured first) | 1073 | ShopEast | 788 | **18** | 671 | **85.2 %** |

**`PeakLive3DVoices` lands exactly ON `SfxLab.PoolSize` (18) in every run, which is SFX-1's own
